Overnight trips, caravans and the open road

Australia on the Move

Australia is big, and most of it can be reached by road. In 2025 Australians took an estimated 17.3 million overnight caravan and camping trips, about 14% more than an adjusted estimate for 2024 (the survey method changed, so treat the rise as approximate). This page follows those trips: where they go, when, how long, and where you can park.

How to read this page

It is written for anyone curious about road trips in Australia, with no travel or statistics knowledge needed. An overnight trip involves spending at least one night away from home. Each chart carries a tag that shows what kind of figures it uses.

Caravan and camping trips

Trips where people stayed in a caravan, tent or campsite. The latest official figures give these for the whole country but not for each state.

All overnight trips

Every overnight trip, whatever the accommodation: holidays, business and family visits. Caravan trips are one part of these totals. A trip that visits two states counts in both.

Sites mapped by volunteers

Caravan parks and campsites that volunteers have added to OpenStreetMap. The map is incomplete, so treat counts as a guide, not a census.

Where and when do Australians travel?

Caravan trips are one slice of Australia’s travel, and official state figures exist only for all overnight trips. So we start with that context: which states attract the most trips for their size, and when the travelling happens.

All overnight trips

Tasmania gets the most trips for its size; Western Australia the fewest

New South Wales receives the most trips overall (39.2 million), but it also has the most people. To compare states fairly, the map shades each one by trips per 1,000 residents: its overnight trips divided by its population. Darker means more trips for its size.

Tasmania leads clearly with 5,068 trips per 1,000 residents, and Western Australia is lowest at 3,208. New South Wales, Queensland, the ACT and Victoria all sit between 4,332 and 4,535, so treat their order as close to a tie.

Visitors from other states count too, so a small state with many visitors scores high. The ACT is too small to see, so it is marked with a ring.

All overnight trips

Most states peak in summer, but the Northern Territory empties out

Each square compares one state’s trips in a three-month period with that state’s own average quarter. Dark blue means busier than usual and red means quieter. Comparing each state with itself reveals seasons that raw totals hide.

The Northern Territory runs 33% above its average in July to September, the dry season, then 32% below in January to March, when the wet season arrives. Tasmania peaks 18% above average in summer.

All overnight trips

Summer stretches trips almost everywhere, but the Territory is the opposite

Each small map shows one season, shaded by the average number of nights per trip. Darker means longer trips. The four maps share one colour scale, so you can compare them directly.

Tasmania’s trips are longest in January to March at 4.0 nights, and Western Australia’s reach 3.8. The Northern Territory runs the other way: 4.8 nights in the dry season (July to September) but 3.9 in the wet.

The ACT is too small to see on these maps. Its trips run 2.6 to 3.0 nights.

How long do trips last, and what do they cost?

Still using all overnight trips as context, these charts compare the states side by side on nights away, trip length and spending.

All overnight trips

New South Wales logs the most nights, and Queensland beats Victoria despite fewer trips

The area of each circle shows the nights visitors spent away in that state, so a circle twice as big means twice as many nights.

New South Wales logged 119.2 million nights, the most of any state. Victoria had more trips than Queensland (31.0 million against 26.0 million) but fewer nights (86.5 million against 92.0 million), because Queensland trips average 3.5 nights to Victoria’s 2.8.

All overnight trips

Trips are shortest in Victoria, the ACT and New South Wales, and longest in the Territory

On a normal map, huge Western Australia swamps the tiny ACT. Here every state gets one equal tile, placed roughly where it sits on the map, with its average trip length printed on it.

The Northern Territory has the longest trips at 4.3 nights. Victoria and the ACT have the shortest at 2.8 nights.

All overnight trips

The Northern Territory is the longest-stay, highest-spending destination

Each line is a state, followed across four measures. Every axis runs from the lowest state at the bottom to the highest at the top, with the real values printed at both ends. All four measures are per trip, per night or per resident, so they describe what a state is like rather than how big it is.

How to read it: follow one line from left to right. Near the top of an axis means that state ranks highest on that measure. Nights per trip, spending per trip and spending per night are linked, so one long-stay state can top all three. Hover over a dot to highlight a state.

The Northern Territory tops trip length (4.3 nights), spending per trip ($1,811) and spending per night ($422). Tasmania leads on trips per resident. Victoria has the cheapest trips at $758 each.

Who goes, and why?

From here the story turns to caravan and camping trips, which the official figures cover for the whole country.

Caravan and camping trips

Four in five caravan and camping trips are holidays

Picture 100 caravan and camping trips. Eighty are holidays, twelve are visits to friends and relatives, and eight are for other reasons.

The average trip lasts 3.4 nights: closer to a long weekend than a grand tour.

Caravan and camping trips

Commercial caravan parks take 67% of trips but 75% of the spending

Each bar splits one measure between commercial caravan parks and every other type of site. Longer stays and paid sites mean more dollars for the parks.

The parks’ share climbs from 67% of trips to 70% of nights to 75% of spending.

Where can you park, and how far is the next site?

Finally, the practical question for anyone towing a caravan: where the parks and campsites are, how far apart they sit, and how many there are for the trips each state receives.

Sites mapped by volunteers

Sites cluster along the coast and thin out across the outback

Each dot is a caravan park (dark red) or a campsite (blue-grey) from OpenStreetMap: 9,782 sites in all, of which 7,767 are campsites. Away from the coast there are far fewer mapped sites, although part of that gap is simply fewer volunteers mapping the outback.

Use the buttons under the map to show only caravan parks or only campsites. OpenStreetMap is mapped by volunteers, so it misses some sites and includes some free bush camps.

Sites mapped by volunteers

About one square in eight is more than 100 km from the nearest mapped site

Dots pile on top of each other along the coast, so they hide how far apart sites really are. Here the land is split into squares about 50 km wide, and each square is coloured by the distance from its middle to the nearest mapped site. Darker red means a longer drive to the next site.

Roughly six in ten squares are more than 25 km from a mapped site, and about one in eight is more than 100 km. The most remote spot, in the Gibson Desert of Western Australia, is about 250 km from the nearest one.

Squares sit on a grid over land only, so the coastline looks slightly ragged. Part of the gap reflects where volunteers have mapped less.

Two sources combined

Per million trips, the Northern Territory has more than six times as many mapped sites as New South Wales

This chart combines two sources: the sites each state has in OpenStreetMap, and the overnight trips it receives, from Tourism Research Australia. Dividing one by the other gives a rough sense of how much camping and caravan accommodation exists for each million trips.

The Northern Territory has 402 mapped sites per million trips, Tasmania 156 and South Australia 153. New South Wales has 62, Victoria 66 and Queensland 77, all below the Australian average of 82.

Trips include every type of stay, not only caravan trips, and OpenStreetMap is incomplete, so read the bars as a rough guide. The ACT has only 25 mapped sites.

Simplified routes

On the Great Ocean Road sites are everywhere; across the Nullarbor they are scarce

The map follows four classic drives and colours every stretch by how many mapped sites lie within 50 km of it, per 100 km of route. Darker blue means more sites. “Rest of the Big Lap” covers the rest of the loop, without repeating the Nullarbor or the Stuart Highway.

The Great Ocean Road has 47 mapped sites per 100 km, against 7 on the Stuart Highway and 8 on the Nullarbor. The emptiest stretch is Tennant Creek to Camooweal, with just 11 mapped sites along 412 km.

Routes are straight lines between towns, not exact roads, so real distances are longer and real site counts per 100 km are lower. Each site is counted once, for its nearest stretch, and OpenStreetMap misses some roadhouse campgrounds, so real gaps are probably shorter than shown.

What the road tells us

Where

Small states punch above their weight. Tasmania sees 5,068 trips per 1,000 residents, against 3,208 in Western Australia.

When and how long

Seasons and distance shape the trip. The Northern Territory has the longest stays (4.3 nights) and Victoria and the ACT the shortest (2.8).

Why and where to park

Four in five caravan and camping trips are holidays. Sites are plentiful on the coast, with 47 per 100 km on the Great Ocean Road, but scarce inland, with 8 on the Nullarbor.
